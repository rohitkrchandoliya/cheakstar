Password Habits, Part Two: The Cleanup Most People Skip

4 Nov 2026 · Cheakstar Blog

You did the hard part already

If you followed the first article in this series, your important accounts now have unique passwords, a password manager remembers them, and two-factor authentication stands guard. That puts you ahead of most people on the internet.

But there is a second layer most guides never mention: the mess around your passwords. Old accounts you forgot, recovery options pointing at an email you no longer control, backup codes saved in a photo you cannot find. Attackers look for exactly these side doors. This cleanup closes them.

Step 1: Close the accounts you no longer use

Every forgotten account is a stored copy of your email address, your details and sometimes your payment information, guarded by a password you set years ago and may have reused since. Make a list of services you signed up for and stopped using - old shopping sites, forums, games, trials. Log in one last time and delete the account properly, or at least change its password to a unique generated one inside your password manager.

An account you closed cannot leak. An account you forgot still can.

Step 2: Fix your recovery paths

Account recovery is the official back door, and it is only as strong as what it points to. Check the recovery email and phone number on your main accounts. The recovery email should be an account that is itself protected with a unique password and two-factor authentication - ideally your most carefully guarded account of all. Remove recovery options you no longer recognise or no longer control, such as a college email that has expired.

If someone takes over your recovery email, they can reset everything else. Treat it like the master key it is.

Step 3: Put your backup codes somewhere real

When you switched on two-factor authentication, each service offered backup codes for the day you lose your phone. Most people screenshot them and forget them, or skip them entirely - then get locked out at the worst moment. Save the codes inside your password manager, in the notes field of the matching account entry. If you prefer paper, print them and keep them somewhere physically safe at home. What matters is that future you can actually find them.

Step 4: Retire your browser's saved passwords

Browsers offer to save passwords, and most people accept for years. Those saved lists are a single point of failure: anyone using your unlocked device, or any malware that reaches it, can often export the whole list in seconds. Move what matters into your password manager, confirm each login works from there, and then clear the browser's stored passwords and turn the offer off. One vault, properly guarded, beats copies scattered everywhere.

Step 5: Make it a fifteen-minute habit

Security drifts. New accounts appear, recovery options age, codes get used. Once every few months, give your password manager a short review: delete dead entries, check the important accounts still have two-factor on, and confirm your recovery details are current. Fifteen minutes, a few times a year.

Part one made your passwords strong. This cleanup makes the ground under them solid. Together, they are the whole habit - and almost nobody who follows both is worth an attacker's time.
