The Six-Digit Code Scam: How Messaging Accounts Get Stolen

6 Nov 2026 · Cheakstar Blog

It starts with a message that feels normal

The message often comes from a number you know. Your cousin, your friend, your colleague writes something like: "I accidentally sent my login code to your number. Please forward it to me, it is urgent." Moments later, a six-digit code really does arrive on your phone.

What has actually happened: the scammer first took over your contact's account, and is now using it to register your number on a new device. The code on your phone is the keys to your account. Forward it, and within minutes your messaging account is theirs - and your contacts start receiving the same request from you.

Why this scam spreads so fast

It borrows trust. People who would never share a code with a stranger share it instantly with a friend in apparent trouble. And it chains: every stolen account becomes the bait for the next ten. Whole friend circles and family groups have fallen in a single evening, each victim politely helping the thief.

The follow-up damage varies. Sometimes the account is used to ask contacts for money. Sometimes group chats are flooded with links. Sometimes the number is simply sold on. In every case, recovery is a miserable process that begins with convincing the platform the account is yours.

The one rule that defeats it

A login code is a password. Never share it - with anyone, for any reason.

No genuine situation requires you to forward a code. Messaging apps never ask for it through another person. A bank never asks for it. A friend who genuinely locked themselves out has official recovery routes that do not pass through your phone. The moment any code is requested by another human being, whatever the story, the answer is no - and that answer never needs an explanation.

Add the built-in second lock

Most messaging apps offer an additional PIN or two-step verification setting, separate from the SMS code. Turn it on. With it set, even a stolen code is not enough to register your account elsewhere - the attacker also needs a PIN that never travels by message. Choose a PIN you will remember, and do not reuse your banking PIN.

While you are in the settings, review the list of linked devices. If a session you do not recognise is connected, remove it on the spot.

If you already shared a code

Act quickly and without embarrassment - embarrassment is what slows people down. Re-register the app on your own phone immediately; this usually pushes the intruder out. Warn your contacts from another channel that your account was misused and that money requests are not from you. Switch on the two-step PIN before doing anything else, and check linked devices. Then tell the friend whose account started the chain - they may not know theirs was taken first.

These scams succeed on politeness and hurry. A household rule fixes both: in this family, codes are never shared, and anyone asked understands exactly why the answer is no.
