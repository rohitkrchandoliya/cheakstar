What Does a VPN Actually Do? (And What It Does Not)

22 Nov 2026 · Cheakstar Blog

Start with the road, not the cloak

Forget the advertisements for a moment and picture something simpler. When your device talks to the internet, its messages travel a road: out of your home or the cafe network, through your internet provider, and on to the site you asked for. People along that road can see more than you might expect - which sites you visit, and on unprotected pages, what you send.

A VPN rebuilds one stretch of that road as a private tunnel. Your traffic is encrypted before it leaves your device, travels the tunnel to the VPN company's server, and continues from there. That is the entire mechanism. Everything a VPN can and cannot do follows from it.

What a VPN genuinely does

It encrypts your traffic on local networks. On a cafe or hotel network, other people sharing that network cannot read what you send. This is the VPN's clearest, most honest benefit.

It hides your activity from your internet provider. Your provider sees a single encrypted stream to one server, not the list of sites you visit.

It shows websites a different address. Sites see the VPN server's address and location instead of your home one. This is why VPNs are used to reach content offered in other regions, and why your approximate location is less visible to sites.

What a VPN does not do

It does not make you anonymous. You are still logged into your accounts, still carrying the same cookies, still typing the same passwords into the same pages. A tunnel hides the road; it does not disguise the traveller.

It does not stop phishing or malware. A fake login page reached through a VPN is still a fake login page. The tunnel delivers the lie in perfect privacy.

It does not remove trust - it relocates it. Your provider can no longer see your destinations, but your VPN company now stands where the provider stood. A provider that logs everything and sells it has made your privacy worse, not better. This is the single most important fact in this article.

Judging a VPN provider honestly

Ask three questions. Does the company state a clear no-logging policy, and has it ever been tested - in court records or independent audits? How does it earn money - a service you do not pay for usually earns from you in some other way. And is it honest in its own advertising? A company promising total invisibility is telling you, in advance, how it treats the truth.

Where a VPN fits in a beginner's defences

Below the essentials, above the optional extras. Unique passwords, a password manager and two-factor authentication protect your accounts themselves; a VPN protects the road to them. If your budget or attention is limited, buy the account protections first. Add a reputable VPN when you regularly work on networks you do not own.

Used with clear eyes, a VPN is a good tool. Used as a magic cloak, it becomes the risk it was bought to prevent.
