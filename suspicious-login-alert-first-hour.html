A Suspicious Login Alert? Your First Hour, Step by Step

15 Nov 2026 · Cheakstar Blog

First: do not act inside the alert

Your phone buzzes: "New sign-in to your account from an unfamiliar device." Your instinct is to tap whatever the message offers. Resist it for one minute. Scam messages imitate these alerts precisely because people react fast and tap blindly.

Open the service the official way instead - its real app, or its address typed by you. If there was a genuine sign-in, it will be visible in the account's security or activity page. If nothing appears there, the alert itself was probably the attack.

Step 1: Change the password from a device you trust

If the sign-in was not yours, change the password now - from a device you trust, ideally not the one that might be compromised. Make the new password long, unique and stored in your password manager. Changing the password locks out anyone who got in with the old one.

Step 2: Sign out every other session

Account settings usually offer a list of signed-in devices and a "sign out everywhere" or per-device sign-out option. Use it. A stolen session can stay alive even after a password change on some services, so end every session you do not recognise, then sign back in on your own devices as needed.

Step 3: Turn on two-factor authentication - or reset it

If two-factor authentication was off, switch it on now; this is exactly the attack it exists to stop. If it was already on and someone still got in, treat your second factor as suspect too: reset it, generate fresh backup codes, and store the new codes safely.

Step 4: Check the recovery paths

Attackers who enter an account often change its recovery email or phone number so they can return. Open your account's recovery settings and confirm both are yours. Remove anything you do not recognise. For an email account, also check for forwarding rules or filters you did not create - a hidden rule that quietly copies your mail to a stranger is a classic trick, and it survives password changes.

Step 5: Look for what changed

Review recent account activity: sent messages you did not write, purchases, changed settings, new linked apps or devices. Remove linked apps you do not recognise. If money is involved, contact your bank through its official number and say the words "account takeover" - banks have a process, and starting it early matters.

Step 6: Warn your contacts if it was real

If the account was genuinely used by someone else, tell your contacts through another channel that messages from that account in the last day may not be from you. It feels embarrassing for a moment. It protects your friends from the follow-up scam, which is the attacker's usual second act.

If it was a false alarm

Sometimes the unfamiliar sign-in is you - a new phone, a trip, a work network that routes through another city. Good. Treat it as a free drill: you now know where your session list, recovery settings and two-factor controls live. That familiarity, built calmly today, is worth more than any of these steps performed in panic later.
