Before You Forward It: A One-Minute Habit That Stops Rumours and Scams

14 Nov 2026 · Cheakstar Blog

The forward button is the engine

Most online harm does not spread because millions of people are careless. It spreads one careful, kind person at a time: a warning forwarded to protect the family group, a miracle offer shared so nobody misses out, a shocking claim passed on because it might be true and the cost of sharing feels like zero.

It is not zero. A forward from you carries your trust with it. Your contacts believe the message a little more because you sent it - which is why scammers design content specifically to be forwarded: the audience is recruited as the delivery system.

What forwarding carries, at its worst

Three passengers ride the forward button regularly. The first is the direct scam: the fake store, the prize claim, the investment tip, engineered so the message itself does the selling. The second is the bait message that starts conversations leading to the six-digit code trick and its cousins. The third is pure rumour - health claims, alarming news, accusations against real people - which harms without stealing a single rupee, and which your name then endorses.

The one-minute routine

Before forwarding anything that asks to be forwarded, give it sixty seconds and four questions.

Who is the original source? Not "a friend sent it" - the actual organisation, publication or person who first said it, and can they be found? Messages that begin with authority and end with nobody are telling you something.

Can I find it independently? Search the claim in your own browser. If a real bank warning, government notice or major event exists, it exists outside this message too - on official pages you can reach yourself.

What does it want me to feel? Urgency, outrage, fear and greed are not information; they are levers. The more a message pushes a feeling, the slower it deserves to travel. Our phishing guide makes the same point about messages aimed at you; it applies identically to messages aimed through you.

What does it want me to do? Click, pay, share a code, or simply spread it further. Naming the requested action out loud often ends the spell - most traps cannot survive being described plainly.

Breaking the chain kindly

You will skip some forwards after this routine. A few will feel awkward - the sender meant well. Two habits keep it graceful. First, do not scold; the trap was built by professionals. Second, when you find a claim is false, consider sending the correction to the same group with the same energy as the original. Corrections travel poorly compared to rumours, but a group with one calm checker in it becomes, over time, a hard group to fool.

Platforms label forwarded messages for a reason: they are the messages nobody present actually wrote or verified. Your pause is the verification. Sixty seconds, and the chain ends with you - which is the most useful place any chain can end.
